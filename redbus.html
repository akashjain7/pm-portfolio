Case study 03 · redBus · Rescue

The bus that never came

When an operator cancels last-minute, redBus issues a refund and leaves the passenger to sort out the night alone. I designed Rescue — a seat automatically held on the next bus, a discounted stay when there isn't one, and a paid Journey Guarantee that covers the cost.

01 / THE PROBLEM

The bus doesn't come — and the app's only answer is a refund

One specific moment in the journey where the product abandons the passenger exactly when they need it most.

Context — what redBus is

redBus is India's largest app for booking intercity bus tickets: you pick a route, choose a seat, and pay in the app, while independent bus operators actually run the buses. The same app also books train tickets and hotels, which matters later in this study.

What happens today

Step 1
The operator cancels the trip sometimes hours, sometimes minutes before departure.
→
Step 2
redBus processes a refund to the original payment source.
→
Step 3
That's it. Getting home is now entirely the passenger's problem.

The night it breaks

You're going Delhi → Dehradun on the 2:30 AM bus booked precisely so you reach by morning. It's 2:10, and the boarding point is a service road off the highway: shuttered shops, parked trucks, two other passengers with bags. 2:30 passes. Nothing. You call the operator: no answer. You call again: "it's cancelled." You open the app: your ticket still says CONFIRMED · ON TIME. Then at 2:47 a notification lands: "Your refund of ₹640 has been initiated."

That notification is the product's entire answer. You're standing on a service road at 3 AM with a bag, in a city that isn't yours. The money will arrive in three to five days. The problem is tonight. What you actually need is one of exactly two things:

Why this stays unbuilt

On the dashboard, this trip closes cleanly: cancellation logged, refund processed, case resolved. The stranding never shows up in any metric — the passenger's worst night on the platform is recorded as a solved ticket. Problems the data can't see don't get roadmap slots.

02 / USERS & MARKET

One user, standing on a service road

This isn't a many-persona problem. There is one user — the stranded passenger — and a few situations that raise the stakes.

The user

The Stranded Passenger

Booked a night bus because it lands by morning — for a shift, an exam, a function, a connecting train. Now it's around 3 AM at an informal boarding point, the operator has cancelled, alternatives are thinning by the minute, and the decision window is short. Money back in 3–5 days solves nothing about tonight.

"Get me on the next bus out — or somewhere safe until morning. Fast, and without me having to figure it out alone."

The same user — when the stakes climb

  • A woman travelling alone at night. Safety outranks money entirely; waiting on an empty road is the worst option on the table.
  • A small group or family. Finding three or four seats together on another bus at 3 AM is genuinely hard — late-night inventory is thin, and whatever is left is usually scattered single seats across different buses.
  • A first-time visitor. Doesn't know which areas are safe, where the bus stand is, or what a fair option looks like at 3 AM.

What they need, in order

A confirmed onward seat first. If no bus exists soon, a safe stay for the night. Throughout, clarity fast — what happened, what the options are, what each costs. The refund matters, but it's the floor: it should ride along with every option, never be the only one.

Rough size of the opportunity

Fermi — to validate

redBus books ~200k+ seats a day (a figure it advertises). If even 1–3% of trips fail materially — last-minute cancellation or no-show — that's roughly 2,000–6,000 stranded passengers a day, or 60k–180k a month. Conservative and unvalidated, but the shape is clear: the failure path isn't a rare edge case. It's a daily event at scale, concentrated on night routes where it hurts most.

How everyone else handles a failed journey

ModeWhen the trip failsWhy buses lag
Trains — redRail / IRCTCAlternate Trip: waitlist confirms, or a 3× refundOne central system holds all inventory and truth — easy to guarantee against.
AirlinesRebooking onto the next flight is standard practiceDeep rebooking norms and infrastructure buses don't have.

redBus's unfair advantage

The standalone redBus app already sells buses, trains, and hotels. No one else in bus travel owns all three. A cancelled bus can be rescued with another bus, a train, or a room — without the passenger ever leaving the app. That's the asset this product spends.

03 / SOLUTION

A rescue, not a refund — and a way to pay for it

Four ways to build this were on the table. Three break on economics or on user behaviour. Here's the reasoning, then the design.

What we rejected along the way

Seven approaches were considered and killed before the design below. Most fail on economics, or because the passenger could already do it themselves with the refund.

ApproachVerdictWhy it dies
Faster, bigger refundRejectedMoney in 3–5 days doesn't solve a service road at 3 AM. Settles the transaction, not the stranding.
Just show nearby alternate busesRejectedThe refund already lets anyone search and rebook. Without a confirmed held seat there is no product — it's a search result, not a rescue.
Silently auto-rebook onto the next busRejectedThe alternate may leave at 5 AM from a boarding point 8 km away. Moving someone without asking is presumptuous — the passenger must choose.
Copy redRail's 3× refund modelRejectedStill money, not a seat — and it works on trains only because one central system (IRCTC) holds all inventory. Buses have thousands of disconnected operators.
Free hotel stays for everyone, redBus-fundedRejectedNobody funds a ₹2,000 room on a ₹600 ticket from platform margin. Replaced by a discounted stay, and a capped covered stay for add-on holders.
Cab to a safe locationRejectedThe standalone redBus app has no cab product — buses, trains, and hotels only. Designing around inventory that isn't there.
Aggressively delist unreliable operatorsRejectedSupply is scarce — very few operators run thin routes and small towns at all. Delisting them leaves those passengers with no bus, which is worse than an unreliable one.
Two-tier: free Rescue for all + paid Journey GuaranteeChosenDelivers the one-tap confirmed seat at near-zero platform cost, and lets those who want full certainty buy it — turning a cost centre into a revenue line.

The design — two tiers, one flow

Tier 1 · For every booking · free
redBus Rescue
  • The moment the operator cancels (the same system event that triggers today's refund), the app fires the Rescue flow instead of a silent refund.
  • A best-match seat is auto-held for 10 minutes — same route, nearest departure, similar seat type. The hold is free; the refund transfers to the new ticket and the passenger pays only the fare difference. (With the add-on, that difference is covered too.)
  • A stay option: a partner hotel near the boarding point at a meaningful discount — they suffered because of a booking made on us; the discount is the apology, funded largely by the hotel's own dead 3 AM inventory.
  • Full refund stays one tap away, always.
Tier 2 · Opt-in add-on at checkout · ₹49–119
Journey Guarantee
  • Priority hold — the seat is reserved before the general pool. When a 40-seat bus dies, everyone is chasing the same few alternates.
  • The alternate seat is free — any fare difference is covered, not charged.
  • No bus within ~4 hours → hotel covered up to ₹1,500, plus a confirmed seat on the first morning departure.
  • Neither possible → full fare back plus travel credit. It always pays out something.
  • Not included: you changing your mind — that's Free Cancellation, a separate product covering a different risk.

Does the money work? — illustrative unit economics

The question is simple: if we sell the add-on, does the money collected cover what we pay out? Worked through on 100 protected tickets.

The point: the add-on stays profitable in every single scenario. Even if both stranded passengers need a hotel — the most expensive outcome possible — we keep ₹8,900 of ₹11,900. The unknown that actually matters isn't the payout, it's the cancellation rate: at 2% this is comfortable, and it only gets tight if failures run several times higher. That's the number to validate first.

The free Rescue tier costs almost nothing separately: the alternate seat is paid for by the refund we were going to issue anyway, and the hotel discount comes mostly from the hotel's own empty 3 AM rooms.

Pricing the add-on — the honest read

Even in the worst case above — both passengers needing a hotel — we return only 25% of what we collect, and in the likeliest cases far less. Insurance products typically pay out 40–60%. So ₹119 is either priced too high — which suppresses how many people buy it, and looks predatory to anyone who does the maths — or the cover should be considerably more generous (a higher stay cap, larger credit). My call: hold ₹119 only on late-night, high-risk routes and price regular routes nearer ₹49–79, tuned on real cancellation data. Lots of people buying a fair guarantee beats a rich margin nobody buys.

What ships — and what doesn't

Ships first · MVP
  • Cancellation-triggered Rescue flow on dense night corridors
  • Auto seat-hold + refund transfer + pay-the-difference
  • Discounted partner-hotel option
  • Journey Guarantee pilot at ~₹119 on night bookings
Fast-follow
  • Train option via redRail where a rail parallel exists
  • Group holds — seats kept together
  • Women's safety flow: stay surfaced first late-night, live status share, priority support
  • Route- and hour-based Guarantee pricing
Explicitly out
  • Mid-journey breakdowns — a different problem, different design
  • Free guaranteed recovery for all, platform-funded
  • Selling the Guarantee on routes where no rescue is ever feasible
Scoped for later — prevention

Many cancellations are predictable: operators drop low-occupancy trips, and some routes fail repeatedly. redBus sees occupancy live — it could flag high-risk trips and consolidate passengers before the bus dies, which is cheaper than any rescue. Sequenced after the reactive core proves out.

04 / SUCCESS METRICS

Measuring the rescue

One number tells us the product works; two payoffs tell us the business should care; a few guardrails keep it honest.

Success in one sentence

When an operator cancels, the passenger still gets where they're going — or gets a safe night — through redBus, in one tap, instead of being left with a refund.

1 · The one number I'd watch

North star · Rescue rate
Of all operator-cancelled bookings, the share where the passenger takes an onward option instead of just a refund.
100 cancellations tonight → 62 board the held bus, 8 take the stay = 70% rescue rate.

2 · Why that number matters — two payoffs

Retention: the passengers most likely to churn are the ones a booking just failed — rescue converts the worst experience on the platform into the most loyalty-building one, measured as post-cancellation repeat-booking rate (a number that silently dies today). Revenue: the Journey Guarantee is a new revenue line — attach rate × price − payouts (attach rate = share of bookings that add it on). On 1,000 night bookings at a 20% attach rate and ₹119: ₹23,800 collected, against ₹600–6,000 in claims depending on how they resolve.

3 · The supporting signals

Leading — should improve

  • Hold-to-board conversion — do held seats get taken?
  • Attach rate (share of bookings that add the Guarantee on) — are people willing to pay for certainty?
  • Stay take-rate late-night — is the hotel rung real or decorative?

Guardrails — must not get worse

  • Payout ratio (claims paid ÷ premium collected) — if it climbs past what the price assumed, the add-on is losing money and must be repriced.
  • Operator cancellation rate — must not rise because a safety net now exists.
  • Refund still one tap — we must never bury or slow the refund button to push people into a rescue. The rescue should win because it's the better option, not because the refund got harder to find. Tracked as taps and seconds to reach it.

4 · How I'd test it

  • Pilot on dense night corridors (Delhi–Dehradun, Delhi–Jaipur class routes) rather than a user-level A/B — you don't withhold a safety option from a control group at 3 AM. Compare against matched corridors.
  • Primary read: rescue rate and post-cancellation repeat-booking. Business read: attach rate and payout ratio.
  • Hold rules set up front: pause if the payout ratio breaches what the price assumed; investigate immediately if operator cancellations climb on pilot corridors.
05 / TRADEOFFS & EDGE CASES

The hard parts, honestly

A guarantee is a trust product — it creates its own risks, and it breaks in specific places. Naming them is the job.

Risks & mitigations

Adverse selection

Riskiest routes and hours buy the most protection, draining the pool.
Mitigation: price by route and hour — that's standard insurance, and redBus has the failure data to do it.

Operator moral hazard

"Passengers get rescued anyway" makes cancelling feel cheaper for operators.
Mitigation: cancellation rate feeds a reliability score that affects search ranking — visibility is the one currency every operator cares about.

Rationing rescue by who paid

Priority hold means paying passengers get scarce alternate seats first — everyone was failed equally, but not everyone is helped equally.
Mitigation: the free tier still gets a real held seat, not a suggestion; priority only decides order when supply is short. Worth stating plainly rather than hiding.

Hold feasibility

If seats can't be reliably held at cancellation time, the product collapses into suggestions.
Mitigation: the hold reuses the existing checkout seat-block; prototype this first — it's the kill-or-ship question.

Over-promise on thin routes

Some routes have no alternate bus at 3 AM, ever.
Mitigation: the Guarantee's payout defaults to the stay or refund-plus-credit there — and says so plainly at purchase. Never promise a bus that can't exist.

Edge cases

  • Thin route, no alternate bus. The rescue degrades gracefully: stay → refund + credit. Stated up front when the Guarantee is bought.
  • Woman alone at night. Ordering flips: if the next bus is hours away, the stay surfaces first, with live-status sharing and a priority support line.
  • Passenger late, bus ran fine. No payout — the trigger is the operator's cancellation event, not a claim. This is what keeps the pool honest.
  • Long delay, not a cancellation. Different flow: live ETA plus a choice — wait, or convert to a rescue if the delay crosses a threshold.
  • "Isn't this Free Cancellation?" No — Free Cancellation covers the passenger changing plans; Journey Guarantee covers the operator failing them. Different products, and the checkout copy must keep them apart.
  • Hotel full at 3 AM. Fall back to the next nearest partner property, else credit — the cap already bounds the cost.
