PM Context

What I was solving for

Orthians Polycraft was a D2C memory foam ergonomic accessories brand I co-founded. My role was to own the full product strategy — user research, market analysis, positioning, pricing, and GTM planning.

The problem: Indians spending long hours at desks, in cars, and on couches were developing chronic back and neck pain. The market had players, but none were competing on genuine product quality at an accessible price. The opportunity was to enter at the same price as the market leader with a meaningfully better product — enabled by in-house manufacturing the competition didn't have.

Core thesis: Better product — higher foam density, superior fabric — at the same price as the market leader. Cost savings from in-house manufacturing went into product quality, not margins.

Primary research

What I found — and what it changed

Conducted 15+ user interviews with potential customers across age groups and occupations, combined with Amazon and Flipkart review mining. Treated findings as directional signals given the friends-and-family methodology bias.

Insight 1 — Pricing isn't the barrier, trust is
Most found ₹1,500–₹2,500 acceptable. The hesitation was about whether the product would actually deliver. This reframed the entire positioning — the problem to solve wasn't price, it was credibility.
Insight 2 — Value takes 20–25 days to feel, not 2–3
Users who stuck with the product for 20–25 days reported meaningful pain reduction. Those who quit in week one didn't feel the benefit. The real churn risk wasn't product quality — it was users giving up too early. This directly shaped the return policy, post-purchase communication, and onboarding strategy.
Insight 3 — Aesthetics are a real adoption barrier
Ergonomic products look unfamiliar. The visual first impression is a significant trial hurdle — especially for products visible on desks or in cars. This shaped the decision to invest in premium fabric and visual identity over functional specs alone.
Insight 4 — Shape retention at 6–8 months vs 3–4 months market standard
Long-term users confirmed the foam held shape and fabric stayed intact — a verified outcome from real usage, not a spec on paper. This became the strongest differentiator in positioning and marketing copy.
Strategy

Key decisions and why

Positioning — same price, better product
Research confirmed buyers weren't price-sensitive — they were trust-sensitive. Competing at the market leader's price point with demonstrably better specs (foam density, fabric quality, shape retention) was the clearest differentiation path. Undercutting on price would've signalled lower quality.
Pricing — market-rate with healthy GM
Priced at ₹1,500–₹4,000 — same as the market leader. In-house manufacturing kept COGS at ~₹550 on a ₹2,000 SKU, delivering ~60% gross margin vs the industry average of 35–40%. That headroom funded aggressive Phase 1 marketing without going underwater.
Return policy — 30 days, not 7
Directly informed by the 20–25 day value realization insight. A standard 7-day window would've generated unnecessary returns from users who hadn't felt the product work yet. The 30-day policy was a product decision, not a marketing one.
GTM — marketplaces first, D2C for margin
Amazon and Flipkart as the Phase 1 volume driver — where buyers search first and where reviews compound. D2C website as the margin driver once trust was established. No offline retail: lower fixed cost, faster feedback loops, and the category already had proven online purchase behaviour.

Honest reflection

What I learned

Orthians didn't launch. The work was real, but the reasons it didn't happen taught me more about 0→1 builds than a smooth launch would have.

Why it didn't launch
We had the strategy, the product vision, and the manufacturing backing. What we didn't have was working capital for the first inventory run while simultaneously building the marketing flywheel. D2C requires cash for inventory, ads, and the time it takes reviews and SEO to compound. We underestimated how long the runway needed to be.
What I'd do differently: validate before building
I'd spend the first 30 days selling manually — WhatsApp, Instagram DMs, local offices — before building a website or listing on Amazon. Even 20 paid sales with real feedback would've sharpened the product brief and de-risked the inventory decision. We jumped to infrastructure before validating willingness to pay.
What worked: the positioning thesis was real
The core bet — better product at the same price, enabled by in-house manufacturing — was genuinely differentiated. The market gap was real. The product strategy wasn't the problem. The business infrastructure around it was.
