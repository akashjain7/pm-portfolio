PM Context

The full arc: build, launch, wall, fix

Orthians Polycraft is a D2C memory foam ergonomic accessories brand I co-founded. I led product from day zero: user research, market analysis, positioning, pricing, and go-to-market. In-house manufacturing was our structural advantage, and every product decision in this case study traces back to it.

The problem we built for: Indians spending long hours at desks, in cars, and on couches were developing chronic back and neck pain. The market had players, but nobody was competing on genuine product quality at an accessible price. Our thesis: enter at the market leader's exact price point with a meaningfully better product, funded by manufacturing cost savings the competition didn't have.

Core thesis: better product, same price. Higher foam density and superior fabric at the market leader's price point. Cost savings from in-house manufacturing went into product quality, not margins.

This case study has two chapters. Chapter one: the zero-to-one decisions that took Orthians from idea to launch. Chapter two: what happened when the post-launch numbers broke, and the research-led fix that followed. The second chapter is where I learned the most.

Chapter one · Zero to one

Four decisions that defined the build

The from-scratch phase came down to four calls. Each is listed with the reasoning and the tradeoff, because that's what building from zero actually is: choosing what to give up.

Decision 1: Who to build for. Tier 1 urban, age 20-35
Desk workers and long commuters with rising disposable income and growing back pain. This segment shops online, reads reviews before buying, and pays for quality. The tradeoff: a narrower initial market than mass-market orthopedic, but chasing everyone would have meant pricing and positioning for nobody.
Decision 2: Positioning. Same price, better product
Competitor teardowns and review mining showed the market's complaints clustered on durability and quality, not price. So we matched the market leader's range (₹1,500 to ₹4,000) with demonstrably better specs: higher foam density, superior fabric, longer shape retention. The tradeoff: undercutting on price would have bought faster volume, but in a category where trust is the whole game, a lower price signals lower quality.
Decision 3: Pricing and unit economics
In-house manufacturing kept COGS near ₹550 on a ₹2,000 SKU, roughly 60% gross margin against an industry average of 35 to 40%. That headroom was the plan's engine: it funded marketing without going underwater and left room to absorb the trust-building costs a challenger brand needs. The tradeoff: manufacturing in-house meant heavier fixed costs and capital tied up in capability instead of inventory.
Decision 4: GTM. Marketplaces for volume, D2C for margin
Amazon and Flipkart first, where buyers search and reviews compound. Our own site as the margin driver once trust was established. No offline retail: lower fixed cost, faster feedback loops, and the category already had proven online purchase behaviour. The tradeoff: marketplace dependence meant growth was gated by marketplace trust signals, ratings and reviews. That dependence is exactly where chapter two begins.

Chapter two · Post-launch

Three numbers working against each other

Months into launch, the model started leaking. Returns were high. Average ratings sat below 4.0. Retention was weak. And new purchases flattened, because the GTM bet had made marketplace trust signals our front door: a low rating suppresses conversion and organic rank, and every return burns reverse logistics cost and usually leaves a bad review behind.

The uncomfortable part: the product tested well in hand. So why were buyers who chose us sending it back?

Problem statement: high returns and weak retention were dragging average ratings below 4.0, which capped new-user acquisition. Diagnose why buyers were rejecting a product that outperformed the market, and fix it without changing the price or the product line.
Why returns hurt twice: the 60% gross margin from chapter one was the plan's engine. Every avoidable return handed that margin to reverse logistics, and often traded it for a one-star review on top.
Diagnosis · Primary research

I ran 15+ interviews across three groups: buyers who returned the product, buyers who kept it, and long-term users past six months. On top of that, review mining across our listings and competitors. The sampling skewed toward reachable users, so I treated findings as directional. The pattern was consistent enough to bet on.

Insight 1: Value arrives at day 20-25. The return window closed at day 7.
Users who stayed consistent for 20 to 25 days reported real pain reduction. But the 7-day return window forced a keep-or-return decision in week one, before the product had any chance to prove itself. Buyers were returning defensively, "while I still can." The deadline was manufacturing returns. The product wasn't.
Insight 2: At ₹2,000, look and feel is part of the product
Buyers spending ₹2,000 expected the object itself to feel worth it. Ergonomic products look unfamiliar, and when the first impression read "medical" instead of "premium," doubt set in before the foam got a chance. The first five minutes were shaping the keep-or-return decision as much as the next three weeks.
Insight 3: Durability was our strongest unclaimed asset
Market-standard foam loses shape in 3-4 months. Long-term users confirmed ours held for 8-9 months, a verified outcome from real usage rather than a spec sheet, with headroom to push further through foam quality. We had a durability advantage buyers could not see at purchase time, and we were not guaranteeing it anywhere.
Chapter two · The turnaround

Three policy bets, and what moved

The counterintuitive bet: extending the return window 4x would reduce returns, not increase them. If value lands at day 20-25, a 7-day deadline guarantees defensive exits. Give the product time to prove itself and those exits disappear. The risk was real: more time could mean more returns if the thesis was wrong. So return rate became the guardrail metric, watched weekly.

The old policy asked buyers for a verdict two weeks before the product could deliver one.
Decision 1: Return window extended from 7 to 30 days
Aligned the decision deadline with the value curve. Buyers now judged the product after feeling the benefit, not before. This was a product decision about when users experience value, not a CX generosity gesture.
Decision 2: 1-year warranty
Turned invisible durability into a visible trust signal at purchase time. 8-9 months of verified shape retention gave us the data confidence to underwrite 12 months, and it planted a claim that competitors working with 3-4 month foam could not safely match.
Decision 3: 30-day replacement policy
A damaged or underwhelming first impression now had an instant resolution path that kept the user in the product instead of exiting to a refund. Replacements preserve the customer. Refunds end the relationship. This also de-risked the aesthetics problem: if look and feel disappointed on arrival, we swapped instead of losing the sale.

Reflection

What this taught me

Two chapters, two sets of lessons.

From the build
Your GTM choice picks your master metric
Choosing marketplaces meant ratings became our oxygen. I'd make the same call again, but I'd instrument trust metrics from day one instead of discovering their weight after launch.
Unit economics are strategy, not accounting
The 60% margin wasn't just profit headroom. It was what let us match the leader's price, fund trust-building, and later absorb a 1-year warranty. Pricing, manufacturing, and marketing were one decision wearing three hats.
From the turnaround
Return windows are product decisions, not CX fine print
The window has to match the value curve. A product that proves itself at day 25 with a day-7 deadline will bleed returns no matter how good the foam is. I now treat every policy, deadline, and default as part of the product surface.
Ratings are an acquisition lever, not a vanity metric
On marketplaces, the rating gates conversion and organic rank. Moving it toward 4.0 restarted new-purchase growth without a rupee of extra ad spend. Trust metrics are top-of-funnel metrics.
The product was never the problem
The foam already beat the market. The system around it, the deadlines, guarantees, and first impressions, was misaligned with how users actually experienced value. Some of the highest-leverage product work happens around the product, not inside it.
Looking forward
What I'd do differently
Compress the path to first paying users. Twenty manual sales over WhatsApp and Instagram DMs before full listings would have surfaced the day 20-25 value curve before launch instead of after it, and the 30-day window could have been the launch policy, not the fix.
What I'd do next
Shift review requests from the standard day 3-5 to day 25+, after value lands, so reviews capture the real experience. Add day 7, 14, and 21 check-ins to keep users consistent through the value curve. And push foam R&D past 12 months of shape retention to widen the durability moat, then let the warranty follow it.
